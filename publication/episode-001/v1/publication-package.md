```text
Press Start, and an invisible field inside the box starts shoving water molecules back and forth about 2.5 billion times a second. Here's how that turns into heat — and why it began with a candy bar that melted in an engineer's pocket.

In the mid-1940s, Percy Spencer was working beside a magnetron, the tube that powers radar, when his candy bar melted. He didn't shrug it off: he tested popcorn, then an egg, then concentrated the energy into a small space. This video follows that idea from the 1945 patent and the nearly six-foot-tall Radarange to the countertop oven, then goes inside to show what the waves actually do to your food.

Questions this video answers:
• What happens inside a microwave oven when you press Start?
• Why does a water molecule heat up in a microwave's electric field?
• Is there a "magic frequency" for water? Do microwaves cook from the inside out?
• Why does food heat in hot and cold spots, and what does the turntable fix (and not fix)?
• Why can the plate be scalding while the food is still cold?
```
