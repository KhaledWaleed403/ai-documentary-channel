## Scene 1 · A path that should have turned back · slot 25 s · est. 21.6 s

[on screen: a solid path leaves Earth and climbs toward Jupiter's orbit]
In 1977, a rocket sent Voyager 2 on its way toward Jupiter. `C18`

[on screen: a dashed ghost branch splits off and curves back toward the Sun, labelled "hypothetical: no Jupiter flyby"]
On its own, that launch wasn't enough to escape. Without Jupiter, its path would have curved back toward the Sun, never getting much farther out than Jupiter's distance. `C23`

[on screen: the solid path bends at Jupiter and carries on past Saturn, Uranus and Neptune]
Instead, it bent at Jupiter. Then at Saturn. Then Uranus. Then Neptune. `C20` `C22`

[on screen: "interstellar space, 2018"]
Today, it's in interstellar space. `C29`

## Scene 2 · The question · slot 10 s · est. 6.4 s

[on screen: zoom into the Jupiter bend; title card]
So how can flying past a planet make a spacecraft faster? The answer depends on who's watching.

## Scene 3 · The flyby, seen from the planet · slot 30 s · est. 25.4 s

[on screen: badge VIEW: JUPITER; Jupiter holds still]
Let's watch from the planet first. In this view, Jupiter sits still.

[on screen: the dot falls in; the speed bar rises]
Our spacecraft falls toward it, and speeds up as it drops in.

[on screen: the hairpin around the planet; the bar peaks, then falls]
It swings around, and starts climbing back out. And climbing out, it slows down again. `C03`

[on screen: entry and exit ghost bars side by side, callout "same"]
By the time it's clear, with no engines firing, it's moving about as fast as when it arrived. Measured from Jupiter, the flyby gave it no extra speed. `C01`

## Scene 4 · Same speed, new direction · slot 20 s · est. 15.2 s

[on screen: the velocity arrow rides along; freeze; entry and exit arrows meet at one origin]
But something did change. Look at the arrows. Same length. Different direction.

[on screen: "speed: how fast" / "velocity: how fast and which way"]
That's the difference between speed and velocity. Speed is how fast. Velocity is how fast, and which way. `C05`

[on screen: the turn angle sweeps in]
The flyby left the speed alone. It turned the velocity. `C01`

## Scene 5 · The switch: seen from the Sun · slot 35 s · est. 27.7 s

[on screen: badge flips to VIEW: SUN; Jupiter begins moving along its orbit]
Now let's watch the same flyby from the Sun. And here's what the first view hid: Jupiter isn't standing still. It's moving along its orbit.

[on screen: the hairpin is carried along and unfolds into a long bent arc]
The spacecraft still follows the same kind of curve around Jupiter. But now that curve is moving through space with the planet.

[on screen: white Sun-frame bar ends longer; the greyed Jupiter-frame bar stays level]
Measured from Jupiter, the speed didn't change. Measured from the Sun, the spacecraft leaves faster than it arrived. `C01` `C02`

[on screen: "same flyby"]
Same flyby. Two answers. Both of them right.

## Scene 6 · Adding the arrows · slot 35 s · est. 24.6 s

[on screen: diagram panel; amber Jupiter arrow, cyan arrow tip-to-tail, white resultant]
Here's why. Seen from the Sun, the spacecraft's velocity is two arrows added together. Jupiter's motion, plus the spacecraft's motion relative to Jupiter. `C02`

[on screen: the amber arrow stays put; the cyan arrow rotates; "only this changed"]
During the flyby, Jupiter's arrow barely changes. The spacecraft's arrow keeps its length, but turns.

[on screen: the white resultant redraws, longer, next to its ghost]
Turned to point more along Jupiter's motion, the two arrows add up to a longer one. That longer arrow is the speed gain, measured from the Sun. `C02` `C04`

## Scene 7 · Who pays · slot 25 s · est. 21.6 s

[on screen: two-pan energy balance; the craft's bar rises]
So where does that extra energy come from? Not from nowhere.

[on screen: Jupiter's bar dips by a hair; the magnifier]
The spacecraft's gravity pulls on Jupiter too. As the spacecraft gains orbital energy, Jupiter gives up a tiny amount of its own orbital energy. `C08`

[on screen: Jupiter's orbit carries on unchanged]
Jupiter is so massive that the change is almost imperceptible. Its orbit carries on. `C09`

The spacecraft didn't make speed. It borrowed it. `C08`

## Scene 8 · Behind or in front · slot 25 s · est. 22.3 s

[on screen: split screen, both VIEW: SUN]
Which way it turns decides everything.

[on screen: left panel, passing behind; white bar longer]
Pass behind a moving planet, and you can leave faster, relative to the Sun. `C04`

[on screen: right panel, passing in front; white bar shorter]
Pass in front of it, and the turn works against you. You leave slower. `C04` `C11`

[on screen: tag "Galileo · Io · 90 kg"]
So a flyby can be an accelerator, or a brake. Galileo skimmed past Jupiter's moon Io to shed energy, and saved ninety kilograms of fuel. `C11` `C12`

## Scene 9 · A favourable alignment · slot 50 s · est. 47.0 s

[on screen: outer-planet time-lapse; the counter stops at 1977; "favourable alignment"]
Now for the real thing. In the late nineteen-seventies, the four giant planets drifted into a favourable alignment, one that comes around roughly once every hundred and seventy-five years. `C25`

[on screen: Voyager 2's path leaves Earth]
The alignment made a journey past all four possible. Voyager 2's trajectory kept that door open. And it took it. `C18` `C22`

[on screen: an inset at each planet showing the turn only; encounter tags]
At Jupiter, its path bent toward Saturn. At Saturn, toward Uranus. At Uranus, toward Neptune, which it reached in nineteen eighty-nine. `C20` `C22`

[on screen: Voyager 1's thinner path turns away at Titan]
Its twin, Voyager 1, took a different road. A close pass by Saturn's moon Titan sent it away from any further giant-planet encounters. `C21`

[on screen: one cumulative comparison, launch bar against after-flybys bar, "more than doubled"; the dashed ghost branch returns]
Measured from the Sun, those flybys more than doubled Voyager 2's speed. And that dashed path is where it would have gone without them. `C24` `C23`

## Scene 10 · Why the Sun is hard to reach · slot 30 s · est. 26.9 s

[on screen: Sun and Earth; a long sideways arrow on Earth]
So if a planet can fling you out of the Solar System, falling into the Sun should be easy. It's just falling, after all.

[on screen: "Earth: ~30 km/s sideways (rel. Sun)"]
The catch: Earth is racing sideways around the Sun at about thirty kilometres a second. And so is everything we launch from it. `C13`

[on screen: the probe "aimed" at the Sun curves past it; the dashed aim line]
Aim straight at the Sun, and that sideways speed carries you right past it. To fall in, you have to get rid of it. `C14`

## Scene 11 · Seven Venus flybys · slot 30 s · est. 22.7 s

[on screen: Parker's first orbit (dashed, wide)]
That was the problem facing NASA's Parker Solar Probe. It needed to shed about fifty-three thousand miles per hour of sideways speed. `C15`

[on screen: the first orbit morphs into the final orbit; Venus ticks on the timeline]
So it used the brake. It flew past Venus seven times. `C31`

[on screen: one cumulative "orbital energy rel. Sun" bar falling]
Taken together, those flybys reduced its orbital energy around the Sun, letting its orbit reach closer and closer in. The last one came in November twenty twenty-four. `C31` `C11`

## Scene 12 · Fastest by slowing down · slot 15 s · est. 15.5 s

[on screen: the dot accelerates down the inner arc; labels "24 Dec 2024", "≈ 6.1 million km"]
Here's the twist. With its orbital energy reduced, Parker fell deep toward the Sun. On Christmas Eve, twenty twenty-four, it passed about six million kilometres from the surface, faster than anything humans have built. `C32` `C33`

It got there by slowing down first. `C33`

## Scene 13 · The whole picture · slot 25 s · est. 23.9 s

[on screen: tile 1, frame switch]
So here's the whole trick, in three parts. One: change your view. From the planet, a flyby only turns you. From the Sun, that turn can change your speed. `C01` `C02`

[on screen: tile 2, energy balance]
Two: nothing is created. Orbital energy is transferred between the spacecraft and the planet. `C08`

[on screen: tile 3, behind or in front]
Three: aim decides. Relative to the Sun, pass behind, and you speed up. Pass in front, and you slow down. `C04`

## Scene 14 · Where it leaves us · slot 15 s · est. 11.4 s

[on screen: pull-out to the star field; a light ring expanding toward Voyager 1]
Voyager 1 is still heading out. On the eighteenth of November, twenty twenty-six, it crosses the one-light-day mark. `C30`

[on screen: fade]
A message from home will take almost a day to reach it. `C30`
