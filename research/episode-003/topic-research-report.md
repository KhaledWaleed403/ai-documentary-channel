## 1. Executive summary

**Recommendation: *How Spacecraft Steal Speed From Planets*, gravity assists told in both directions.** The first half shows how flying past a planet can throw a probe out faster (Voyager). The second half shows the counterintuitive reverse: to reach the Sun or Mercury, a spacecraft has to use planets to *brake* (Parker Solar Probe, BepiColombo).

Why this topic:
- **Shorts demand is proven.** Gravity-assist Shorts have 8.5M, 1.4M and 949K views [M].
- **Long-form demand is current.** "Harder to reach the Sun than to leave the Solar System" got 10.75M (Kurzgesagt) and 3.93M (Ridddle) in the last two months [M].
- **It leaves a gap.** Nearly every video explains only speeding up, and most use the misleading "free energy" framing. The two-reference-frame explanation plus the braking half is under-served [M/H].
- **It changes our look.** Orbits, velocity arrows and frame switches replace the cutaway-of-a-machine format both earlier episodes used, without leaving the dark, cyan-and-amber 2D identity.
- **It has two news pegs this month that it doesn't depend on.** Voyager 1 reaches one light-day on 18 Nov 2026, and BepiColombo enters Mercury orbit in late November 2026 [M, reported].

**Runners-up:** *Why Your Phone Needs Einstein* (GPS and relativity, score 82) and *How Headphones Erase Sound* (noise cancelling, score 80). The recommendation scores 88.

**Our own data is tiny.** The channel is 1–3 days old with 1 YouTube subscriber. It supports only one lean [H]: Shorts that open on a contradiction ("Seawater soaks up radio — so how does a hidden submarine get a message?") held viewers better than Shorts that open on a statement. Gravity assists are built on exactly that kind of contradiction.

## 4. Top three finalists

### Finalist A (space / astrophysics): gravity assists — *recommended*

- **Long-form title:** *How Spacecraft Steal Speed From Planets*
  - Alternatives: *Why NASA Flies Toward Planets to Go Faster* / *The Planet Trick That Got Voyager Out of the Solar System*. Run all of them through `yt-package` before publishing.
- **Central question:** A planet's gravity pulls a passing probe in, then pulls it back just as hard on the way out. So how does the probe leave *faster*, and why do missions to the Sun use the same trick to *slow down*?
- **Narrative angle:** Two views of one flyby. From the planet, nothing changes: the probe leaves at the speed it arrived. From the Sun, it has gained the planet's motion. Then reverse it: pass in front of the planet instead, and you lose speed. That is how Parker Solar Probe and BepiColombo get somewhere *harder* to reach than interstellar space. Most competitors show only the speed-up half, and many call it "free energy." We show who pays: Jupiter, by an amount too small to measure.
- **Why viewers care:** It flips intuition twice (falling past a planet makes you faster; getting to the Sun is the hard trip). It's tied to two live events (Voyager at one light-day on 18 Nov, BepiColombo at Mercury in late November). And it gives a reusable model: "fall in, turn, borrow the planet's motion."
- **Opening visual concept:** A single cyan probe dot falls toward a huge amber planet, whips around it and leaves. A speed readout shows the same number in and out. The camera pulls back to the Sun's view, the planet's orbital arrow appears, and the outbound arrow visibly lengthens. The narration states the contradiction in the first line, along the lines of "It arrives at one speed and leaves at the same speed — and it's still faster." (Exact wording at script stage.)
- **Science to explain:**
  - Orbits as falling.
  - Speed as a vector, and adding velocities between frames.
  - Hyperbolic flybys (speed in = speed out relative to the planet; direction bent).
  - Conservation: the planet pays.
  - Flyby geometry: passing behind the planet speeds you up, in front slows you down.
  - Earth's orbital speed as the obstacle to reaching the Sun.
  - Launch windows and planetary alignment.
  - What to leave out: the Oberth effect (mention as "a different trick" at most) and patched-conic maths.
- **Five-plus standalone Shorts:** see §8.
- **Visual sequences:**
  1. Planet-frame flyby on a hyperbola, with a speedometer that stays the same.
  2. Sun-frame overlay, with vector-addition arrows.
  3. Tennis ball bouncing off the front of a moving train: the classic analogy, drawn as a diagram.
  4. "Who pays": Jupiter's mass against the probe's, shown on a log ruler (`ScaleIndicator` reused).
  5. Grand-tour orbital map with the four giants lining up.
  6. Reverse flyby in front of the planet; the arrow shrinks.
  7. Earth's sideways speed arrow, and why aiming straight at the Sun misses.
  8. Parker's seven Venus loops tightening.
  9. BepiColombo's nine flybys as a step-ladder.
  10. A three-beat recap.
- **Research sources:** sources.md A1–A9. Primary: NASA *Basics of Space Flight* Ch. 4-1, the JPL grand-tour poster, NASA Goddard SVS 13017 and ESA BepiColombo.
- **Difficulty compared with Ep 001/002:** **Lower on art, similar on accuracy.** There's no machinery to draw. Trajectories are computed (a hyperbola plus a frame shift), so they are both cheap and correct. The risk is conceptual: vector addition has to be shown clearly, not just stated. About 3 new components (see §9).

### Finalist B (engineering / technology): GPS and relativity

- **Long-form title:** *Why Your Phone Needs Einstein to Find You* (alternative: *GPS Is a Clock in Space, Not a Map*).
- **Central question:** How does a phone work out where it is from satellites that don't know it exists, and why would the system drift without relativity?
- **Narrative angle:** GPS is a timing machine. Satellites broadcast only "what time it is here." Your phone does the geometry, and the clocks overhead tick at the wrong rate for two opposite reasons (speed slows them, weaker gravity speeds them up). The honest limit: the "10 km a day" myth versus the real error (sources.md B2), which competitors repeat uncorrected.
- **Why viewers care:** Everyone uses it, it's invisible, and it is relativity they carry every day. Recent long-form competition is under 3K views.
- **Opening visual:** A phone's blue dot drifting across a city map as a clock overlay counts up microseconds.
- **Concepts:** Trilateration from time delays (light-time spheres), why four satellites are needed (the receiver clock is a fourth unknown), atomic clocks, special-relativity time dilation, gravitational time dilation, and the factory frequency offset.
- **Shorts:**
  1. GPS satellites don't know where you are.
  2. Why your phone needs four satellites, not three.
  3. Clocks in orbit run fast; here is by how much.
  4. The two effects that pull in opposite directions.
  5. The clock set "wrong" on purpose before launch.
- **Visual sequences:** Expanding light-time spheres intersecting; two clock faces drifting apart, with a microsecond counter; a gravity "well" depth against orbital speed as two opposing gauges.
- **Sources:** sources.md B1–B3 (Ashby 2003; Pogge, OSU).
- **Difficulty:** Medium. Spheres intersecting in 3D need careful 2D cross-sections, and the relativity wording needs a careful fact-check. It overlaps thematically with Ep 002's No-GPS Short (which also gives a ready cross-link).

### Finalist C (accessible physics): noise-cancelling headphones

- **Long-form title:** *How Headphones Erase Sound — and Why They Can't Erase Voices*
- **Central question:** How can adding more sound make things quieter, and why does it silence an engine but not a conversation?
- **Narrative angle:** Sound is a pressure wave, and a mirror-image wave cancels it. Then the catch: cancellation only works where the wave is long compared with the gap between microphone and ear, and in time. Hums are long waves; voices are short ones, so passive foam does that job.
- **Why viewers care:** Many people own a pair. The Shorts appetite is enormous (9M, 7.2M, 3.4M).
- **Opening visual:** Two waves sliding together into a flat line, as the engine hum drops away.
- **Concepts:** Pressure waves, superposition and phase, feed-forward and feedback microphones, latency, wavelength against cancellation-zone size, passive isolation, and transparency mode.
- **Shorts:**
  1. Adding sound to make silence.
  2. Why it can't cancel voices.
  3. The microphones you never see.
  4. Why ANC can feel like pressure in your ears (needs a source).
  5. How transparency mode works.
- **Visual sequences:** A wave-addition bench, cross-section of an ear cup with mic, speaker and ear, and a frequency ruler showing the active and passive zones.
- **Sources:** sources.md C1–C2. A headphone-engineering source is still needed.
- **Difficulty:** Low to medium. But it is the third wave-heavy episode in a row (§2.3), and the "voices" angle is currently farmed by low-effort uploads.

## 9. Production considerations (existing Remotion workflow)

- **Pipeline unchanged:** research → `claims.md` (stage 2) → script → storyboard → Kokoro `af_heart` narration → animation → review → publish (`docs/production-workflow.md`). Folders: `research/episode-003/`, `episodes/episode-003/<version>/`, `src/scenes/episode003/`, `src/compositions/Episode003*.tsx`.
- **New reusable components** (add each to `docs/component-inventory.md` when built):
  - `OrbitPath`: draws a conic (ellipse or hyperbola) from orbital parameters and moves a body along it by frame. The geometry is computed, so it is accurate and deterministic.
