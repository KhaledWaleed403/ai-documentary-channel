### Crew and enclosure

**CREW-01 — VERIFIED (High).**
- *Evidence:* NAVY-SSN [read]: Virginia class "Crew: 145: 17 officers; 128 enlisted". NAVY-SSBN [read]: Ohio "Crew: 15 Officers, 144 Enlisted". NRC-INTRO p. 11 [read]: "The nuclear-powered attack submarines have a designated crew of about 130 men…". Table 1-1 [read]: SSBN "2 rotating crews, 160 men per crew".
- *Scope:* US nuclear submarines. The NRC figures are from around 2003.
- *Safe wording:* "A US nuclear submarine carries well over a hundred people — 145 on a Virginia-class boat."
- *Visual:* "145" only with a US Virginia-class label.

**CREW-02 — QUALIFIED (Med-high).**
- *Evidence:*
  - NAVY-SSBN [read]: "On average, the submarines spend 77 days at sea followed by 35 days in-port for maintenance."
  - NRC-INTRO Table 1-1 [read]: SSBN "patrols >60 days in length"; SSN "Irregular intervals between patrols; patrols of variable length".
  - NRC-WASTE p. 41 [read]: "All U.S. submarines are nuclear-powered and have the ability to stay submerged for entire missions lasting up to several months."
- *Scope:* US. "At sea" is not the same as "submerged throughout".
- *Safe wording:* "For weeks or even months at a time."

**CREW-03 — VERIFIED (High).**
- *Evidence:* NRC-INTRO pp. 11–12 [read]: "When submerged, a submarine is an enclosed and isolated environment." / "When the submarine is submerged, air is recirculated in a closed-loop system."

- *On-screen OK:* "+1 atm per ~10 m (33 ft) of seawater".

Failure modes assessed: "shell yielding, axisymmetric shell buckling, asymmetric shell buckling, overall buckling and buckling of end closure." - *Visual:* inward arrows all round a circular cross-section; ring frames; inner circle labelled "pressure hull". No collapse animation implying real failure depths.

**PRES-03 — REJECTED.** No operating depth is published (NAVY-SSN and NAVY-SSBN [read] list none). Drop the car analogy, since no defensible calculation ties it to a real depth.

**PRES-04 — QUALIFIED (illustrative).** Label any flat-versus-round panel "simplified".

### Carbon dioxide

**CO2-01 — REJECTED.** No source supports a universal "CO₂ first" sequence. NRC-INTRO and IMAREST treat oxygen supply and CO₂ removal as separate controls.

**CO2-02 — VERIFIED (High).**
- *Evidence:* NRC-CO2 p. 47 [read]: "Submarine crew are reported to be the major source of CO2 on board submarines (Crawl 2003)."

**CO2-03 — VERIFIED (High)** for measured averages, with the outdoor comparison qualified.
- *Evidence:* NRC-CO2 p. 47 [read]: "Data collected on nine nuclear-powered ballistic missile submarines indicate an average CO2 concentration of 3,500 ppm with a range of 0-10,600 ppm, and data collected on 10 nuclear-powered attack submarines indicate an average CO2 concentration of 4,100 ppm with a range of 300-11,300 ppm (Hagar 2003)."
- *What the numbers are:* **measured concentrations** (averages and ranges), not limits. US nuclear subs, data reported in 2003.
- *Outdoor comparison:* IMAREST [read] gives normal air as "0.04 per cent. carbon dioxide" (≈400 ppm). Present-day outdoor CO₂ is a little higher; that figure is not sourced here.
- *Safe wording:* "Measurements on US nuclear submarines found carbon dioxide averaging around 3,500 to 4,100 parts per million — several times the level in outdoor air."
- *On-screen OK:* "3,500–4,100 ppm (average, US subs, 2003 data)". Don't print "10×".

**CO2-04 — VERIFIED (High).**
- *Evidence (NRC-CO2 [read]):*
  - p. 47: "CO2 is a simple asphyxiant and lethal asphyxiations have been reported at concentrations as low as 110,000 ppm (Hamilton and Hardy 1974). Loss of consciousness can occur within a minute of exposure at 300,000 ppm and within 5-10 minutes (min) of exposure at 100,000 ppm (HSDB 2004)."
  - p. 60: a lowest-observed-adverse-effect level of 25,000 ppm "on the basis of subtle and asymptomatic errors of visual tracking and depth perception".
  - Dyspnoea at higher levels, e.g. "At 40,000-50,000 ppm for 17-32 min, 16 subjects reported dyspnea".
- *Safe wording:* "In high enough concentrations, carbon dioxide causes breathlessness and headaches, and at extreme levels unconsciousness. Submarines keep it far below those levels."
- *On-screen:* prefer a qualitative scale. If numbers are used: "~25,000 ppm: first subtle effects measured" / "100,000 ppm: unconsciousness within minutes" (both [read]).

**CO2-05 — QUALIFIED (Med-high).**
- *Evidence:*
  - NRC-INTRO p. 11 [read]: "Monoethanolamine scrubbers are used to remove carbon dioxide from the air."
  - IMAREST [read], RN-hist: CO₂ is absorbed by amine, released on heating, "compressed and discharged overboard".
  - COLLINS [read]: the newer ACRU "uses a new solid amine system".
- *Scope:* The overboard step is documented for the RN, historically. US-2007 confirms MEA scrubbers but doesn't describe the release step.
- *Safe wording:* as v1 ("…pumped out of the boat") with "in classic designs" if challenged.

**CO2-06 — QUALIFIED.** IMAREST [read], RN-hist: soda-lime canisters for "patrol submarines" (conventional boats) and for escape.

**CO2-07 — VERIFIED.** NRC-CO2 p. 46 [read]: "CO2 is heavier than air, and that contributes to the development of toxic exposure situations in enclosed spaces."

**CO2-08 — (new) VERIFIED (High). What the Navy numbers are.**
- *Evidence:*
  - NRC-INTRO [read]: EEGLs are "ceiling concentrations (concentrations not to be exceeded) of chemical substances that will not cause irreversible harm to crew health or prevent the performance of essential tasks… during rare emergency situations lasting 1-24 h."
  - CEGLs are "ceiling concentrations… designed to prevent the immediate or delayed adverse health effects or degradations in crew performance that might result from continuous chemical exposures lasting up to 90 days."
  - NRC-CO2 Table 3-3, p. 60 [read]: EEGL 1 h — Navy current 40,000 / proposed 30,000 / NRC recommended 25,000 ppm. EEGL 24 h — 40,000 / 15,000 / 25,000. CEGL 90 days — 5,000 / 7,000 / 8,000.
  - Basis for the 90-day 8,000 ppm recommendation [read]: the 25,000 ppm LOAEL "adjusting it with an uncertainty factor of 3".
- *These are:* **exposure guidance ceilings** (emergency and continuous), not operational set-points or measured levels. The "current" and "proposed" Navy values are as of the 2007 report; what the Navy adopted afterwards is not established here.
- *Safe wording:* "Navy guidance has set long-term limits of a few thousand parts per million." Or omit the numbers.
- *On-screen:* avoid limit numbers. If needed: "Navy 90-day guidance: 5,000 ppm (as reviewed in 2007)".

### Oxygen

- *Evidence:* COLLINS [read]: supplies oxygen generation "for every operational class of U.S. submarines"; units "use a Proton Exchange Membrane (PEM) Electrolysis Cell stack to produce low pressure oxygen via electrolysis of water." NRC-INTRO p. 11 [read]: "Oxygen generators add oxygen to the air through the electrolysis of seawater."

**O2-02 — VERIFIED for US-2007 (Med-high).**
- *Evidence:* NRC-INTRO p. 11 [read]: "The hydrogen that is generated in the process is discharged to the sea." IMAREST [read], RN-hist: "the hydrogen being discharged overboard."
- *Not established:* whether current Collins PEM units do the same. COLLINS [read] is silent on hydrogen handling. A vendor (ACI) claims an electrolyser design with no gas rejected overboard [via search, v1].
- *Safe wording:* "The leftover hydrogen is explosive in enough quantity, so it's sent out of the boat into the sea."
- *Visual:* H₂ routed out through the hull is acceptable.

**O2-03 — QUALIFIED.** IMAREST [read], RN-hist only. Optional.

**O2-04 — QUALIFIED (Low-med). Sources conflict.**
- *Evidence:* IMAREST [read]: "Demineralised water, to which an electrolyte has been added". NRC-INTRO [read]: "electrolysis of seawater". COLLINS [read]: "electrolysis of water" (unspecified).
- *Safe wording:* "It splits water" — don't specify seawater or purified water.
- *Visual:* show the electrolyser fed from a generic internal water line. Don't show raw ocean intake, and don't label the feed "purified".

**O2-05 — QUALIFIED.** IMAREST [read], RN-hist: aim 20–21% O₂; fire-risk ceiling ≈22%. No current US set-points. Keep the meter qualitative.

**O2-06 — VERIFIED.** NRC-INTRO p. 11 [read] lists oxygen generators and MEA scrubbers as separate methods.

### Other atmosphere control

**ATM-01 — VERIFIED (High).**
- *Evidence:* NRC-INTRO pp. 11–12 [read]: carbon monoxide and hydrogen "are removed using a carbon monoxide-hydrogen burner that catalytically oxidizes the two components…". Activated charcoal filters are named for galley and sanitary exhaust.

**ATM-02 — VERIFIED (High).**
- *Evidence:* NRC-INTRO p. 13 [read]: "The submarine atmosphere is monitored with the central atmosphere monitoring system (CAMS), which uses an infrared spectrometer to measure carbon monoxide and a mass spectrometer to measure oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, hydrogen, water vapor, and Freon…", and "Fan room air is monitored continuously, and air in other onboard locations is analyzed on a rotating basis."
- COLLINS [read]: CAMS IIA uses "mass spectrometry and near infrared technology".

**ATM-03 — VERIFIED (High), scope US-2007.**
- *Evidence:* NRC-INTRO p. 12 [read]: the forward compartment air-circulation system "contains most of the air-purification equipment and oxygen generators and is designed to condition the air to 80°F and 50% relative humidity." The engine-room system "provides heating, cooling, and air distribution… designed to maintain room air temperature below a maximum of 100°F."
- *Scope:* design targets for a US nuclear attack submarine as described to the NRC (citing NAVSEA, 2003). Actual conditions vary.
- *Safe wording:* "The same system air-conditions the boat, designed for about 80°F (27°C) and 50% humidity in the living spaces."
- *On-screen OK:* "Design target: ~80°F / 50% RH" with a "US attack sub" label.

**ATM-04 — (new) VERIFIED (High), scope US-2007.**
- *Evidence:* NRC-INTRO p. 11 [read]: "Major sources of air contaminants on a submarine include cigarette smoking, cooking, and the human body. Other sources include control equipment, the power train, weapons systems, batteries, sanitary tanks, air-conditioning and refrigeration systems, and a variety of maintenance and repair activities."
- *Safe wording:* "Cooking, batteries, machinery and the crew themselves all release other gases into the air." Don't mention weapons systems (out of scope).

### Water, waste and food

**H2O-01 / H2O-02 — QUALIFIED (Medium).**
- *Evidence:* NAVPERS-16170 §10E [read], US-1955:
  - "The discussion herein will be confined solely to the type to be found on submarines with nuclear steam plants… Sea water is heated to the point of vaporization, condensed to make fresh water and the resulting brine pumped overboard. The heat source of the vapor compressor is electricity whereas the heat source of the Soloshell is steam."
  - §10E2: "This distilling plant is of the low pressure, two effect, Soloshell type. The rated capacity of the two-effect unit is 4,000 gallons per day…"
  - Ch. 10 intro [read]: the other new (non-nuclear) designs "employ the vapor compression principle and all are entirely electric."
- *Scope:* US Navy, 1955: the first nuclear-powered submarines and contemporary diesel boats. **No source found for the current US (Virginia, Ohio, Columbia) water-making method or capacity.** Secondary sources mention reverse osmosis on newer boats; a Royal Navy RO prototype for Vanguard was reported [via search, v1]. The OSTI "submarine reverse osmosis" report (Peters, 1980) concerns undersea *desalination plants*, not vessels — **not applicable**.
- *Safe wording:* "Submarines make their own fresh water from the sea. US Navy manuals from the first nuclear subs describe boiling seawater under low pressure with steam from the plant, condensing the vapour, and pumping the salty leftovers overboard." Don't state today's method or capacity.
- *Visual:* low-pressure evaporator → condenser → fresh-water tank, with brine overboard. Label it "classic method" or similar. **Don't show "4,000 gallons/day"** as a current figure (it's US-1955).

**H2O-03 — REJECTED.** No source links fresh-water production to reactor coolant. NT-26-2 [read] describes the reactor coolant as a closed system.

**WASTE-01 — VERIFIED (High), scope US-1996.**
- *Evidence:* NRC-WASTE p. 41 [read]: "Dry waste is consolidated using a trash compactor and then placed in special cans… Metal weights are added to ensure that the cans will go to the bottom. The cans are ejected from the submarine using a trash disposal unit (TDU), which is a long cylindrical, vertical tube connected to the ocean through a ball valve. Several cans are placed atop one another in the TDU, the top of the TDU is sealed by closing a pressure cap, the ball valve is opened, and the cans ejected through a combination of gravity and air pressure." Cans are "28.5 inches long and 9 inches in diameter."
- *Safe wording:* "Rubbish is crushed into weighted steel cans and dropped out through a sealed tube in the hull, so it sinks."
